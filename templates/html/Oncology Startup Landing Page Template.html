The thesis: cancer care needs a computational layer.

Oncology fails when we treat correlation as causation: the same mutation and the same regimen can produce different outcomes because the biological state differs. The missing layer is a model that connects state → intervention → outcome — and improves with every case.

State

Tumor biology compiled into a longitudinal state — not a static report — across genomics, imaging, labs, and clinical context.

Intervention

Therapies, combinations, dose changes, and supportive care, represented as structured interventions over the state.

Outcome

Response, progression, toxicity, and survival captured as updates to the state — closing the learning loop.

“Biology is observed, but rarely modeled.” Cancer care needs models that learn, not just records that store.

The problem: personalization breaks at the point of care.

Clinicians must decide across exploding therapies, biomarkers, and lines of treatment — but the patient’s biology is not compiled into a single state they can reason over.

Hospitals have data, but not a learning loop.

Outcomes rarely flow back into a model that updates future decisions. Systems record what happened, but they don’t compute how biology and therapy interacted — so the system repeats the same uncertainty and the cost…

Fragmented context at tumor board.

Weeks of scans, notes, and reports sit in different systems. The oncologist has minutes to reconstruct a mental model of the patient’s biology — without a computational substrate.

Approach: from biology to decision.

AlphaGrid is structured as three coordinated layers: a biological state model (the “twin”), a patient context graph (the “compiler”), and multi‑agent orchestration (the “decision” layer).

5.1 · Twin layer

Biological state modeling.

State is not a snapshot; it is a timeline of observations + inferred latent factors. As new tests arrive (scan, biopsy, markers, adverse events), the model updates the patient state and its uncertainty.

The twin stores why something is believed (traceable evidence) and what would change the belief (missing tests / next best information).

5.2 · Compiler layer

Context graph — making the patient queryable.

The graph exposes nodes (findings, biomarkers, lesions, therapies, events) and edges (causal/clinical relations, temporal dependencies).

Reasoning happens over the graph: which changes explain progression? which therapy constraints apply? what evidence supports a pivot? Messy charts become a structured substrate for simulation and decision support.

5.3 · Decision layer

Multi‑agent orchestration.

Specialized agents handle data extraction/normalization, cohort matching, guideline parsing, evidence retrieval, toxicity & interaction checks, and plan synthesis.

A final “clinician‑in‑the‑loop” agent produces a decision memo with traceability: what evidence was used, what assumptions were made, and what alternatives were rejected.

Why now.

Therapy space is expanding faster than human cognition and clinic time: combinations, biomarkers, and sequential lines increase decision complexity far beyond what manual mental models can track.

Tooling in most hospitals is a system of record, not a system of reasoning — it stores documents, but cannot compute over biology.

Foundation models and multimodal representation learning make it newly feasible to represent complex tumor microenvironments and map them to outcomes — the same shift that enabled modern digital biology.
