Biological Significance

Cell division is the process by which a parent cell divides into two or more daughter cells. It is fundamental for:

  • Growth: Increasing the number of cells in an organism.
  • Repair: Replacing damaged or dead cells (e.g., skin healing).
  • Reproduction: Asexual reproduction in single-celled organisms.

Interphase: Preparation

Not part of Mitosis

Often confused as a "resting phase," Interphase is actually a period of intense activity where the cell grows, synthesizes proteins, and replicates its DNA to prepare for division. It accounts for about 90% of the cell cycle.

Cell Cycle Checkpoints

Specific points where the cell cycle stops until a go-ahead signal is received. These prevent uncontrolled division (cancer).

G1 Checkpoint (Restriction Point)

The most important checkpoint. Located at the end of G1.

Is cell size adequate?
Are nutrients sufficient?
Is DNA undamaged?

If failed: Cell enters G0 (resting state).

G2 Checkpoint

Occurs at the end of G2, before Mitosis begins.

Was DNA replication successful?
Is DNA undamaged?

If failed: Cell cycle halts to attempt repair. If irreparable, apoptosis (cell death) occurs.

M Checkpoint (Spindle Checkpoint)

Occurs during Metaphase.

Are all chromosomes attached to spindle fibers?

Consequence: Ensures daughter cells don't end up with missing or extra chromosomes.

Quick Review

1. In which phase do sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles?

Correct! Spindle fibers shorten to pull them apart.

2. What happens if the G1 checkpoint fails but the cell continues to divide?

Correct. Bypassing checkpoints is a hallmark of cancer cells.
