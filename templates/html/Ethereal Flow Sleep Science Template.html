The science behind the method

Built on established sleep, psychological and behavioural science.

The science is not decoration. It shapes the order of the experience.

01

UNDERSTAND THE LOOP

Pre-sleep cognitive arousal — worry, rumination, planning, replaying and monitoring — is associated with difficulty switching off.

02

PREPARE THE STATE

Slow breathing, relaxation and body-settling can support physiological down-regulation and create a better starting state.

03

CHANGE THE RESPONSE

Established psychological sleep approaches work with thoughts, beliefs, worry and responses rather than relying only on passive relaxation.

04

REHEARSE THE ALTERNATIVE

Guided imagery and mental rehearsal give attention a coherent direction and can support practising alternative responses.

05

REPEAT + SLEEP CONSOLIDATION

Repeated practice supports learning. Sleep is a natural biological environment for memory processing and consolidation — without implying that ideas are “programmed” into an unconscious brain.

06

PERSONALISE + SEQUENCE

Your answers help determine which evidence-informed ingredients, pacing, language, imagery and sequence are most relevant to your pattern.

Being physically tired does not automatically mean the mind is ready to disengage.
You do not need to stop every thought. You need a different response when the same thought comes back.
You do not just think differently. You practise experiencing the situation differently.

Evidence

The research underneath.

The method draws on established research across sleep science, psychology, cognitive behavioural research, relaxation, guided imagery, learning and memory. Below are some of the key sources that inform the approach.

Pre-sleep cognitive arousalLemyre A, Belzile F, Landry M, Bastien CH, Beaudoin LP. “Pre-sleep cognitive activity in adults: A systematic review.” Sleep Medicine Reviews. 2020;50:101253. PMID 31918338.

Systematic-review evidence links insomnia and difficulty initiating sleep with greater sleep-interfering thoughts, worry, monitoring, planning and cognitive arousal.
Slow breathing + autonomic regulationLaborde S, et al. “Effects of voluntary slow breathing on heart rate and heart rate variability: A systematic review and a meta-analysis.” Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews. 2022;138:104711. PMID 35623448.

A large systematic review and meta-analysis found measurable effects of slow breathing on autonomic regulation, including vagally mediated heart-rate variability.
Progressive relaxation + body settlingDonato KO, et al. “Progressive muscle relaxation technique improves sleep quality and mental health: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.” Journal of Psychosomatic Research. 2026. PMID 41633054.

Meta-analytic evidence across randomised trials supports progressive muscle relaxation as a sleep-support and body-settling technique.
Cognitive, mindfulness + acceptance componentsFurukawa Y, et al. “Components and Delivery Formats of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Chronic Insomnia in Adults: A Systematic Review and Component Network Meta-Analysis.” JAMA Psychiatry. 2024;81(4):357–365. PMID 38231522.
Supporting: Riemann D, et al. “The European Insomnia Guideline: An update on the diagnosis and treatment of insomnia 2023.” Journal of Sleep Research. 2023. PMID 38016484.

Large-scale evidence shows that cognitive and acceptance-based components are important parts of established psychological approaches to persistent sleep difficulty.
Guided imagery + mental simulationHallford DJ, et al. “Synthesizing the effects of mental simulation on behavior change: Systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis.” 2021. PMID 33948918.
Supporting: Harvey AG, Payne S. “The management of unwanted pre-sleep thoughts in insomnia: distraction with imagery versus general distraction.” Behaviour Research and Therapy. 2002;40(3):267–277. PMID 11863237.

Mental-simulation research shows reliable effects on behaviour, while sleep-specific imagery research supports imagery as an alternative focus for unwanted pre-sleep cognitive activity.
Sleep + memory consolidationDiekelmann S, Born J. “The memory function of sleep.” Nature Reviews Neuroscience. 2010;11:114–126. PMID 20046194.
Supporting: Brodt S, Inostroza M, Niethard N, Born J. “Sleep—A brain-state serving systems memory consolidation.” Neuron. 2023;111(7):1050–1075. PMID 37023710.

Foundational and contemporary neuroscience reviews describe sleep as an active period of memory consolidation, replay and reorganisation.
